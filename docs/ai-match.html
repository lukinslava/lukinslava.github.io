AI Match

An internal AI tool for a design agency that hires designers. It reads CVs and portfolios, sorts them out and matches people to a role: instead of a base of 5,577, the design lead gets a working pool of 30–60.

The problem

The agency's base holds 5,577 designers — from Huntflow, from Behance, from referrals. On paper that is wealth. In practice it was a dump: roles tagged at random, grades out of touch with reality, portfolios nobody had opened since the day they were uploaded.

When a vacancy came in, the recruiter searched from memory and by keywords, and the design lead went through portfolios by hand — dozens of links for one role. Half of them missed: wrong grade, wrong domain, a “product designer” who had been drawing banners for five years.

The brief was simple: a working pool of 30–60 people instead of 5,577, where most are genuinely worth a look.

Three decisions

Half the product grew out of these.

01

Precision over recall

The tool does not have to find everyone who fits. It has to not waste time on people who don't. If the first ten hold fewer than three strong candidates, the product is useless, however much it found.

02

A human in the loop

The model is not equally good at everything. On portfolio visuals it is poor: we checked three times — on an abstract 0–100 scale it hugs the top and gives everyone 70–90, and the same work scores differently across runs. So the visual grade is set by the design lead; the model only pulls the interface screens out of the portfolio and puts them next to reference ones. Where the model is reliably wrong, we don't show its number at all.

03

Cheap narrowing first, AI last

Running the whole base through a model is slow and expensive. So selection is staged: role and grade are filtered in the database, instantly; everyone who passes is checked against the vacancy in code, with no model, and gets a score you can unfold requirement by requirement; only the best 30 go to the model.

How it works

A vacancy from anything

The recruiter pastes the client correspondence, uploads a PDF or a recording of the briefing — and gets a filled-in vacancy. Fields the model is unsure about are flagged: “AI isn't sure — check this.”

Matching

One button, and about three minutes later the pool is ready: a score against the vacancy criteria, strengths, gaps, questions to ask, and a ready first message to the candidate. Below it sits “also a fit by data” — people the model never read, but who are close on the numbers.

The candidate card

A scraper walks the portfolio — the homepage and up to eight cases, picked by how much they look like a case rather than by their order on the page. The model reads the CV and the work: role, grade, skills, domains, seven portfolio scales, strengths and gaps. The card is split into tabs: overview, portfolio, experience, notes.

Pipeline and the client shortlist

Approved people move onto a board of stages, from the internal shortlist to an offer. Days on a stage turn red when someone is stuck. The client gets the shortlist by link — no account — and answers right there: “let's interview”, “not sure”.

The public review

The product has a second side. A designer sends a link to their portfolio and, a few minutes later, gets a review written with the same logic the design lead sees: what works, what is missing, case by case.

The review is one text for two readers — the internal document is written in the third person, and a separate pass rewrites it for the designer, in second person. While it runs, the page honestly shows the queue — “2 people ahead of you” — instead of an endless spinner. Every request lands in the same base: the agency gets candidates, the designer gets an honest outside look.

So far 146 designers have sent their portfolio through the public form.

How it was built

There was no development team: the product was built by one designer together with AI agents. Mine are the framing, the decisions and the checking of what came back; the code is written by agents from specs and plans we draft together.

Economics became part of the design

The expensive part is the model reading a portfolio, so the product spends money on a schedule. During the day a candidate is parsed on demand; the queue from Huntflow runs at night, in batches through the Batch API — half the price, about two cents per person. Parsing the whole base costs around a hundred dollars.

Two lessons that became rules in the code

26 September

The account ran out of money in the middle of a night run, and 802 people were left with “parsing failed” — dropping out of the queue for good.

→ Hopeless errors — money, quota — now keep a person in the queue.

2 October

Google's file storage filled up, and 507 of 567 requests went through at full price.

→ At night we never pay full price in any outcome: if batches aren't accepted, the night stops and waits.

A weekend on its own

From Friday evening to Monday the parsing ran on its own, with nobody watching. As of Sunday night: 1,121 people, $23, not a single full-price request.

Where it landed

5,577designers in the base — tagged by role, grade, skills, domains and portfolio scores
30–60people in the pool a design lead reads for one vacancy
~2¢per candidate parsed

Of the base, 5,450 people are parsed and tagged — 127 are still in the queue. The tool is still rolling out across the agency, so there are no numbers yet for time per vacancy or hires made through it.

Everything on screen is demo data on a local copy of the product: all people, companies, vacancies and portfolios are invented, and no real candidate, client or third-party work appears on any frame.
